Reverse Mortgage Guide by Life Situation

For BC homeowners 55+ with plenty of home equity but tight monthly cash flow. When a reverse mortgage fits, when it does not, and what the alternatives are.

Estimate my limit →

Reverse mortgages in three points

Cash without selling

At 55+, you can receive part of your home’s value (usually up to 55%, or 59 to 60% with higher-limit products for 70+) as a lump sum or scheduled advances. The home stays in your name.

No required payments

It is repaid when you sell, when the last owner dies, or when you move into care. Meanwhile, interest keeps adding to the balance.

No tax, no effect on benefits

The money is a loan, not income, so it is not taxed and does not reduce OAS or GIS. Property tax, insurance and upkeep stay your responsibility.

The biggest myth: “the bank takes the house when you die”

Not true. The home stays in your name, and whatever is left after the loan is repaid goes to your heirs.

  • You keep ownership: a reverse mortgage is a loan secured by your home, not a sale to the bank. Title does not change.
  • Only the loan balance is repaid: when the last owner dies, the lender receives the principal and interest owing at that time. The rest of the home’s value belongs to your heirs (your estate).
  • Your heirs choose: they can sell the home, repay the balance and keep the rest, or repay it with other money or a new mortgage and keep the home. Lenders usually allow several months (about 180 days to a year, depending on the lender) to settle.
  • Never more than the home is worth: as long as the home was kept up (property tax and insurance paid), you never owe more than its value, even if the balance grows past it (the no negative equity guarantee). No debt passes to your heirs.

Example: at 70, you take $440,000 on a $1M home. Twenty years later, with 3% yearly growth the home is worth about $1.8M and the balance is about $1.5M. Selling the home repays the loan and about $280,000 goes to your heirs. The amount left depends on rates and home prices.

Eight situations and how it can help

The people below are hypothetical examples. Amounts are calculator estimates; actual limits are set by appraisal and underwriting.

1. Retired, but the mortgage is still there

Example · 68, detached home in Burnaby worth $1.4M, $280,000 left on the mortgage, payments are hard on a pension

How it can help
Paying off the mortgage with a reverse mortgage ends the monthly payment. A reverse mortgage must be in first position, so existing secured debt is paid off at closing anyway.
Watch for
Reverse mortgage rates are usually higher than regular renewal rates. If the balance is small and you can still qualify on income, a regular renewal may cost less.
Alternatives
Renew with a lender that can qualify you and extend the amortization, or use a reverse mortgage for only part of the balance.

2. Delaying CPP to 70

Example · couple aged 65 and 63, townhouse in Coquitlam worth $1.1M, no mortgage

How it can help
CPP rises 0.7% for each month you wait after 65, or 42% at 70, for life. Scheduled reverse mortgage advances can cover living costs for those five years. The money is not income, so OAS is not affected.
Watch for
Limits are low in the early-to-mid 60s (roughly 20 to 40% of home value). Take only what you need as scheduled advances so less interest builds up, and weigh your health and life expectancy.
Alternatives
Open a HELOC before retiring and use it as a bridge (see 8), or draw RRSPs first to even out your tax brackets.

3. Staying in the home, but it needs changes

Example · 76, living alone, detached home in Victoria worth $1.0M, needs a stairlift and bathroom changes

How it can help
Take a lump sum for the renovation and draw more later if needed. The biggest benefit is staying in a familiar home and neighbourhood.
Related support
The BC home renovation tax credit for seniors and persons with disabilities (10% of costs, up to $1,000 a year) and the federal Home Accessibility Tax Credit (HATC) can be claimed as well.
Watch for
Think about future care needs or a move. If you may sell within a few years, prepayment charges can apply.

4. Helping a child with a first down payment

Example · couple aged 72 and 70, detached home in Richmond worth $1.8M, child is preparing to buy a first home

How it can help
This gives part of an inheritance early. A bigger down payment can lower your child’s mortgage insurance and monthly payment.
Watch for
Interest keeps growing on the money you give, so less equity is left later. Think about fairness between siblings and the gift letter your child’s lender will ask for.
Alternatives
Joint ownership or a family loan instead of a gift, or your child’s FHSA and RRSP Home Buyers’ Plan.
Try these numbers →

5. Credit card and line of credit debt is squeezing the budget

Example · 70, detached home in Surrey worth $1.3M, $90,000 on cards and lines of credit

How it can help
Pay off high-interest debt at once and end the minimum payments. This is most useful when retirement income is too low to qualify for a regular consolidation loan.
Watch for
If the cause of the debt (spending, supporting family) does not change, it can build up again. All debt secured on the home must be paid off from the reverse mortgage.
Alternatives
If you can qualify on income, a regular refinance or HELOC costs less.

6. Needing care at home

Example · couple aged 80 and 78, detached home in Kelowna worth $950,000, one spouse needs home care

How it can help
Use scheduled or as-needed advances for caregivers and help at home. Limits are higher at older ages (usually around 55%), so more is available.
Watch for
If both of you move into care, the loan becomes due. If one moves and the other stays, it usually continues (check with the lender).
Alternatives
BC’s property tax deferment program (55+) to ease property tax, and government home support services.
Try these numbers →

7. Living alone after losing a spouse

Example · 74, living alone, detached home in Nanaimo worth $850,000, one pension stopped and income fell short

How it can help
Top up the lower income with scheduled advances and stay in the home, buying time instead of selling in a hurry.
Watch for
Avoid big decisions in a rush. Apply for survivor benefits first (such as the CPP survivor’s pension) and talk about estate plans with your children.
Alternatives
Downsize to a smaller home or condo, or rent out a room or secondary suite.
Try these numbers →

8. Before retiring: set up a HELOC early

Example · 60, still working, detached home in Langley worth $1.5M, retiring in 2 to 3 years

How it can help
Opening a large HELOC while you still have income gives you a lower-rate emergency fund or bridge after retiring, when qualifying on income gets hard. In your early 60s a HELOC limit (up to 65% of home value) is bigger than a reverse mortgage.
Watch for
A HELOC needs at least interest payments every month, has a variable rate, and the lender can reduce or freeze the limit. If paying the interest becomes hard, a reverse mortgage can pay off the HELOC later.

When it fits, and when to think twice

Often a good fit

  • You plan to stay in your home for a long time
  • Your income is too low for a regular loan
  • You need cash without monthly payments
  • You are 70+ and the limit is large enough
  • Living well now matters more than the size of the estate

Think twice if

  • You may sell within a few years (prepayment charges)
  • You need a small amount and can qualify on income (a HELOC costs less)
  • Passing the home to your children intact matters most
  • You are around 60 and the limit is too small
  • Property tax, insurance and upkeep are hard to afford

Steps and costs

  1. Consultation and estimate (a broker compares several lenders)
  2. Application and conditional approval (usually a few days)
  3. Appraisal and signing the commitment
  4. Signing with the closing lawyer and the independent legal advice (ILA) lawyer
  5. Funding: your mortgage is paid off and the rest is deposited (usually 3 to 6 weeks from application)

Typical costs: lender set-up fee about $995 + legal about $1,500 (registration about $1,000 + ILA about $500) + appraisal. In November 2026, 5-year fixed rates were about 6.2 to 6.4%, and higher-limit products for 70+ started around 7.4%. Prepayment charges are highest in the first years and shrink over time.
