The U.S. and Canada Diverge: A Fed Hike Is a Given, and the Loonie Slips Below 70 Cents

Published Thursday, November 19, 2026 · Rates, market figures and outlooks in this article reflect its publication date and may differ today. Ask us for the latest picture. Translated from the Korean original.

Right after last week's U.S. midterm elections, bond yields, oil and the U.S. dollar all fell together, giving Canada's rate market a rare breather, but that move did not last a week. As U.S. inflation came in higher than expected and Fed officials made a string of hawkish comments, U.S. Treasury yields and the dollar climbed again, while in Canada inflation held steady and the job market weakened, so the two countries' rate paths have begun to diverge.

Key Points

  • U.S. inflation picks up again, and a December Fed hike is now a given: U.S. consumer prices rose 0.4% in October from the previous month, above the 0.3% forecast, and core inflation rose 0.3% for a third straight month. With Fed officials adding hawkish comments, including Governor Waller raising the possibility of a 0.5 percentage point hike, the market-implied probability of a December hike rose from about 80% right after the election to around 93–96%.
  • U.S. Treasury yields back above 5%, and the dollar at a one-year high: The U.S. 10-year Treasury yield, which fell to 4.85% right after the election, rose as high as 5.17% intraday on the 13th and is now trading around 5.06%. Demand at that week's 10-year and 30-year Treasury auctions was also weak. The U.S. Dollar Index reached around 103.8, its highest level in about a year.
  • Steady inflation and weak jobs in Canada, with BoC December hike odds at about even: Canada's October CPI was 3.1%, the same as September and below the 3.2% forecast; inflation excluding energy was 2.0%, and core inflation actually eased to around 1.8–2.0%. Combined with three straight months of job losses, the swap market's probability of another BoC hike in December, which had been around 60%, has come down to about even.
  • Diverging rate paths push the loonie below 70 cents: With a U.S. hike all but certain and expectations of a Canadian hike fading, the interest rate gap between the two countries has widened again. The Canadian dollar, at 1.3961 per U.S. dollar (about 71.6 cents) right after the election, slid to 1.4326 (about 69.8 cents) on the 18th, giving back all of its post-election rebound.

What It Means

In the end, last week's breather was short-lived. Following U.S. Treasury yields, Canada's 5-year bond yield also rose from 3.35% on the 9th to 3.52% on the 18th, moving back above 3.5%. However, because Canada's own inflation and employment data are weak, Canadian yields rose less than U.S. yields, and rather than jumping sharply again, fixed rates look more likely to move up and down around current levels while facing upward pressure. With oil staying in the US$80–90 range on a Brent basis, energy-driven inflation pressure has eased somewhat, but the risk that a prolonged weak loonie could reignite core inflation through import prices is what the BoC will watch most closely. The direction from here depends on the Fed meeting on December 8–9, the BoC meeting on December 9, and the U.S. November CPI release on December 10. In particular, with the BoC's December decision finely balanced between a hold and a hike, Canadian employment data released in the meantime and the exchange rate will be the most important variables for variable-rate borrowers.
