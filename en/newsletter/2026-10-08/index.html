Talk of Lifting the Foreign Homebuyer Ban in 2027: The Real Variable Driving the Housing Market

Published Thursday, October 8, 2026 · Rates, market figures and outlooks in this article reflect its publication date and may differ today. Ask us for the latest picture. Translated from the Korean original.

The federal government's "Foreign Homebuyer Ban," introduced in 2023 to cool home prices that overheated during the pandemic, is now under review ahead of its expiry in 2027. The government says it will consider a range of options, including easing the rules, to reflect changing market conditions. But at a time when the big interest rate cycle and bond market supply and demand dominate everything, the effect of such micro-level regulatory changes on mortgage rates and real buying power needs to be reassessed very soberly.

Key Points

  • The dilemma of lifting the rules and attracting capital: The federal government is reviewing either fully lifting the foreign buyer ban, set to expire in 2027, or a compromise (such as allowing new builds). This is largely a last-ditch measure to inject foreign capital into a housing supply market choked by high rates.
  • The "real cause of the decline" hidden behind an illusion: The key reason Canadian home prices fell about 20% from their peak over the past two years or so was not the foreign buyer rules, but the surge in the "cost of capital" caused by the Bank of Canada's (BoC) policy rate hikes and the spike in global long-term bond yields.
  • A paradigm shift in the housing market: In the era of near-zero rates, speculative foreign capital was a major variable moving home prices, but now that we have entered a "Higher for Longer" phase, there are major structural limits to producing explosive asset gains like in the past, even if some foreign buying resumes.
  • The BoC's "inflation defence" stance and the risk of conflict: If an inflow of foreign capital re-ignites short-term real estate asset inflation, it could backfire by giving the Bank of Canada — which is weighing tightening to defend against import prices driven by U.S. tariffs and high oil prices — one more justification for a "rate hike."

What It Means

This review of the foreign homebuyer ban may stir some investor sentiment in the housing market, but it cannot cover up the fundamental "risk of entrenched high rates" that we face.

What currently has a grip on Canada's real estate market is not whether foreign investors return, but the direction of the rapidly climbing U.S. 10-year Treasury yield and the Government of Canada 5-year bond yield that is rising alongside it under that pressure. No matter how much foreign money flows in to support the pre-sale market, if the joint tightening warnings from North America's central banks and the surge in long-term bond yields don't let up and fixed mortgage rates settle at high levels, the real buying power of most domestic buyers will struggle to recover.

So if you need financing, rather than being distracted by the government's easing of real estate rules, you should focus on the massive wave of tightening in global financial markets. When renewing your mortgage or buying a home, we strongly recommend being wary of vague hopes for a market recovery from lifted regulations or an early rate cut, and making it your top priority to build a steady household cash-flow defence line through a rate hold.
