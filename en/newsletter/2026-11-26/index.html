Macklem: "The Target Is 2% Total Inflation" — Odds of a December BoC Hike Jump to 80%

Published Thursday, November 26, 2026 · Rates, market figures and outlooks in this article reflect its publication date and may differ today. Ask us for the latest picture. Translated from the Korean original.

On November 24, Bank of Canada (BoC) Governor Tiff Macklem laid out the principles that will guide monetary policy going forward in a speech to the Economic Club of Canada in Toronto. The Governor made no commitment about the December rate decision, but markets took it as a signal of another hike, and the probability of a December hike, which was about even a week earlier, jumped to around 80%. Focusing on the speech, we look at why the chance of a hike has risen even though core inflation has eased.

Key Points

  • The target is "2% total inflation," and core inflation is a reference indicator: Earlier this month, the government and the BoC agreed to keep the 2% inflation target for another five years. Governor Macklem made clear that the target is total consumer price inflation (CPI), including food and gasoline, and that core inflation is only a reference indicator for gauging where inflation is headed, not the target. Among core measures, the Bank will now rely a little more on CPI-trim (inflation after cutting off the items with the largest price changes up and down each month) and CPIX (inflation excluding eight volatile items such as gasoline, fruit, vegetables and mortgage interest), and it also launched a new dashboard bringing together a range of inflation indicators.
  • A warning about the "second-round effects" of high oil prices: Inflation in Canada has been around 3% since April. With oil prices and refinery margins down recently, year-end inflation may not be as high as the October forecast (3.5%), but businesses have so far absorbed the production and transport costs that have built up since the spring, and the Bank expects more of these to be passed on to consumer prices in the year ahead. Its view is that these second-round effects could keep inflation elevated for longer.
  • A more resilient economy than expected, reaffirming the case for the October hike: He re-emphasized that the greater risk of persistent inflation, together with an economy holding up better than expected, was the reason for the October hike (to 2.50%). He did not mention the December decision, but markets took this as a signal that the rationale for the October hike still holds.
  • Market reaction: December hike odds go from about even to around 80%: The probability of a December BoC hike, which had fallen below 50% right after the October CPI release, rose to 80–85% in the swap market after the speech. The Canadian dollar also rebounded against the U.S. dollar, from 1.4369 on the 23rd to 1.4219 on the 25th. By contrast, Canada's 5-year bond yield, the benchmark for fixed rates, fell from 3.53% on the 19th to 3.41% on the 25th as a slide in U.S. AI-related stocks drove investors toward safe-haven assets.

What It Means

Expectations of another BoC hike, which had cooled last week on softer core inflation and weak employment, were revived by a single speech from the Governor. The key is what the BoC bases its judgment on. Even if core inflation is steady at around 2%, if total inflation stays in the 3% range and the high oil-price costs that have built up risk spreading to consumer prices next year, that is a reason to raise rates. As a result, the chance that variable rates rise by another 0.25 percentage point on December 9 has increased. Interestingly, fixed rates are moving the other way. With the slide in U.S. tech stocks sending money into safe-haven government bonds, the 5-year yield has actually come down, so variable rates now face upward pressure while fixed rates face downward pressure at the same time. However, the speech was not a commitment on the December decision, so if Canada's third-quarter GDP and November employment data, both due before the meeting, come in weaker than expected, the odds of a hike could shift again. Developments in the Middle East, the trade conflict with the United States, and whether the slide in U.S. stocks spills over into the real economy are also variables to watch.
