U.S. Long Bonds Break 5.2% as the U.S. and Japan Activate the FIMA Repo: Rising Rates and the Line of Defence for the Mortgage Market

Published Thursday, August 6, 2026 · Rates, market figures and outlooks in this article reflect its publication date and may differ today. Ask us for the latest picture. Translated from the Korean original.

With U.S. 20- and 30-year long-term Treasury yields topping 5.2% and hitting their highest level in more than 20 years, joint yen intervention by U.S. and Japanese authorities has now been followed by active use of the Fed's FIMA repo facility. An all-out effort is underway to keep Japan from dumping U.S. Treasuries and to calm the rate tantrum, but the powerful upward trend in global long-term bond yields is putting heavy upward pressure on Canadian fixed mortgage rates as well.

Key Points

  • U.S. long-term yields at a 20-year high: U.S. 20-year and 30-year Treasury yields broke above 5.2%, the highest level in more than 20 years.
  • Joint U.S.–Japan intervention, deliberately made visible: Japanese Finance Minister Katayama and U.S. Treasury Secretary Bessent carried out joint yen purchases of $5–10 billion and deliberately let the market know about it.
  • Raising ammunition through the FIMA repo facility: Rather than selling its U.S. Treasury holdings in the market to raise dollars, Japan pledged Treasuries as collateral with the Fed and borrowed dollars (FIMA Repo), defending the bond market against collapse.
  • Ongoing Treasury supply pressure: Despite the authorities' carefully executed currency intervention and the repo backstop, the structural limit has become clear—the massive U.S. fiscal deficit and the burden of new Treasury issuance mean the rise in long-term yields will not easily be reversed.
  • Canadian fixed rates moving in step: As U.S. long-term yields, the global benchmark, keep rising, upward pressure is building on the linked Government of Canada 5-year bond yield (GoC 5Y) and on fixed mortgage rates.

What It Means

If Japan had simply dumped its huge U.S. Treasury holdings on the market to buy yen, Treasury yields would have soared uncontrollably beyond today's 5.2%. Fortunately, by using the Fed's "FIMA repo facility" to raise dollars by pledging Treasuries as collateral rather than selling them, the worst-case market panic and rate spike are being very effectively prevented.

But this has only kept the dam from breaking—the water level is already very high. Unless the underlying problem of massive Treasury supply driven by U.S. fiscal deficits is resolved, Canadian banks' funding costs will have no choice but to stay elevated. If you have an upcoming mortgage renewal or home purchase, we recommend that you re-examine your debt-repayment capacity relative to your income and your cash flow very conservatively and carefully, given the external volatility that could shake rates again at any time.
