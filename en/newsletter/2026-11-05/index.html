Democrats Sweep the U.S. Midterms as Oil Plunges and the Dollar Weakens: A Breather for Canadian Bond Yields and Fixed Rates

Published Thursday, November 5, 2026 · Rates, market figures and outlooks in this article reflect its publication date and may differ today. Ask us for the latest picture. Translated from the Korean original.

In the U.S. midterm elections held on November 3, Democrats took back both the House and the Senate, sending financial markets swinging. Stock index futures plunged right after the results came in and then rebounded, while U.S. Treasury yields, international oil prices and the U.S. dollar all fell at the same time. We look at what this macro backdrop, which has shifted in the week since last week's BoC hike and Fed hold, means for Canada's bond market and mortgage rates.

Key Points

  • Democrats sweep both chambers, entering a "checks and balances" phase: Democrats secured both a Senate majority and a House majority (counting continues in some close races). Markets read this as a phase in which the administration's policies will be checked by Congress for the next two years; AI and chip stocks weakened on concerns about tighter regulation, while health care and alternative energy stocks rallied.
  • Oil plunges for a fourth straight day: Brent crude, which was near US$118 a barrel last Friday, fell 8.7% on November 4 alone to the US$94 range, and WTI dropped to around US$88, the lowest levels since August. This reflects both the view that the election results reduce the risk of military conflict with Iran and a recovery in shipping volumes through the Strait of Hormuz.
  • U.S. Treasury yields fall, dollar weakens: The U.S. 10-year Treasury yield, which was 5.05% on the day of the Fed hold, came down to around 4.85%, its lowest since September 10. The U.S. dollar index also slipped to its lowest level since the end of September.
  • Loonie at a seven-week high, Canadian bond yields fall too: Despite the oil plunge, the weaker U.S. dollar had a bigger effect, and the Canadian dollar hit a seven-week high at 1.3965 (about 71.6 cents). Canada's 10-year yield fell 7 bp in a day to 3.71%, its lowest since late August, and the 5-year yield also dropped below 3.5%.

What It Means

This week's moves are a rare favourable combination for Canada's rate market. The core rationale for last week's BoC hike was "the risk of high oil prices spreading to inflation broadly," yet oil has fallen nearly 20% in a single week, and a stronger Canadian dollar also eases pressure on import prices. The probability of another hike in December, which the swap market put at around 45% right after the hike, now has room to come down. With the 5-year yield, the benchmark for fixed rates, falling along with U.S. Treasuries, upward pressure on fixed rates looks likely to ease for the time being. Still, it is too early to call a trend reversal. Markets still put the odds of a U.S. Fed hike in December at around 80%, and Friday's U.S. jobs data could change this picture again. There are also warnings that if Iran risk flares up again, Brent could return to US$110–125. In addition, lower oil prices cut both ways for Canada as an energy exporter, weighing on its income and investment. Variable-rate borrowers should watch whether pressure for a further hike eases at the BoC's December 9 meeting, and those weighing a fixed rate should watch whether the 5-year yield settles below 3.5%.
