Canada's Surprise Trade Surplus: An Illusion Created by Gold and Crude Oil, and Where Rates Are Headed

Published Thursday, May 7, 2026 · Rates, market figures and outlooks in this article reflect its publication date and may differ today. Ask us for the latest picture. Translated from the Korean original.

As recent geopolitical instability in the Middle East sent international gold and oil prices soaring, Canada posted a surprise trade surplus for the first time in six months. However, this came not from higher export volumes but from higher prices, and it is sending mixed signals for the mortgage market going forward.

Key Points

  • Surprise swing to a trade surplus: the March trade balance far beat market expectations of a deficit, posting a $1.8 billion surplus and escaping the deficit trap for the first time in six months
  • Surge in gold and crude oil exports: global uncertainty, including the Iran conflict, drove a sharp jump in the value of safe-haven metals and energy (crude oil) exports, leading the overall surplus
  • Actual trade volumes declined: despite the surplus, actual export volumes fell 0.3% and consumer goods imports also declined, showing Canada's domestic economy itself somewhat contracting
  • Calm bond yield reaction: once it was clear this was a nominal price effect rather than an overheating economy, Canada's 2-year government bond yield actually edged down right after the release and stabilized

What It Means

Rising international oil prices slow the decline in inflation, which lowers expectations of early rate cuts by the Bank of Canada (BoC). However, with the domestic real economy in a slump, government bond yields are also unlikely to spike on internal factors, so for the time being fixed mortgage rates are expected to move 'sideways within a range' without large swings, following their recent rise.
