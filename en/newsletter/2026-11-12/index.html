Federal Budget 2026: A Focus on Building More Homes, and What Changes for Your Mortgage

Published Thursday, November 12, 2026 · Rates, market figures and outlooks in this article reflect its publication date and may differ today. Ask us for the latest picture. Translated from the Korean original.

The housing policy in the 2026 federal budget, tabled on November 5, comes down to one goal: getting more homes built. It adds $13 billion over 10 years to Build Canada Homes, which builds affordable housing under government leadership; extends the ban on foreign purchases of existing homes to 2029 while exempting newly built homes; and widens access to financing so small-scale builders can more easily build three- and four-unit homes. Below, we explain in plain terms only the changes that directly affect people buying a home or facing a mortgage renewal.

Key Points

  • Home price limit for bank-insured loans with 20% or more down rises from $1 million to $1.5 million (proposed): When you put down 20% or more, there is a type of loan (insurable) where the bank can insure it at its own cost without the borrower paying a premium, but today this is only possible when the home price is under $1 million. The budget proposes raising this limit to $1.5 million and allowing amortizations of up to 30 years if at least one borrower is a first-time home buyer (FTHB) or the home is newly built. Because it is still only a proposal, no start date has been set.
  • An easy point to confuse: loans with less than 20% down are already at $1.5 million: Loans where the down payment is under 20% and the borrower pays mortgage insurance premiums (insured) have already been available for homes under $1.5 million since December 15, 2024. This proposal would bring loans with 20% or more down in line with the same limit.
  • Newly built three- and four-unit homes can be financed up to 90% of the property's value (effective February 1, 2027): When building a new three- or four-unit home, the owner will be able to get an insured loan for up to 90% of the property's value, with no maximum property value and no requirement to live in one of the units. Once built, the property can be paid off over 30 years while being rented out or sold unit by unit, so this mainly applies to builders and investors.
  • Easier rate comparison and income verification: The Bank of Canada (BoC) will publish more detailed information on insured mortgage rates, and the government plans to consult industry on making prepayment penalties, the fees charged when you pay off a mortgage early, easier to understand. The Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) also plans to pilot a service from 2027-28 that verifies borrowers' income directly for lenders, which could make preparing income documents simpler over time.

What It Means

For borrowers, the first item is the one to watch most closely, but its effect is more limited than it may seem. Rates on these loans step down gradually as the down payment ratio rises: putting down 30% already lowers the rate noticeably, and the best terms usually come at around 35% or more. That structure is not changing; this proposal widens the range of home prices eligible for these rates from $1 million to $1.5 million, increasing the number of people who qualify. For example, if you buy a $1.2 million home with 35% or more down, you cannot get this rate today because of the price limit, but you would be able to in the future. And if it is your first home or a newly built home, a 30-year amortization can lower your monthly payment. However, a longer amortization means more total interest, and the stress test applied when qualifying for a mortgage (checking your ability to pay at the higher of your contract rate plus 2% or 5.25%) remains in place. Most of the other measures are aimed at getting more new homes built, so they are unlikely to change current home prices or market activity much in the short term. Above all, this change to the price limit is still a proposal with no start date, so rather than putting off a purchase or renewal while waiting for it, it is worth first comparing the terms available to you today.
